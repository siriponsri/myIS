Beyond the Retriever: Representation Transfer in
Cross-Domain Patent Retrieval
Anonymous Authors

Abstract—A patent retriever does not observe an invention
directly; it scores a constructed representation of that invention.
When benchmark comparisons hold the indexed representation
fixed, the resulting differences can be interpreted as properties
of the retrievers. We test this interpretation on family-level
cross-domain patent retrieval by freezing five heterogeneous
retrieval systems and varying deterministic document constructions. Development experiments show heterogeneous responses
to representation search. In a cross-retriever transfer matrix,
the nominally best representation source changes across consuming retrievers, although within-target Recall@100 differences
are small and target identity remains strongly associated with
absolute performance. We treat this as descriptive evidence
about portability and evaluate the selected complete system
separately. On 872 held-out queries, the selected configuration
improves Recall@100 from 0.331097 to 0.442476 (paired difference 0.111379, 95% bootstrap CI [0.102294, 0.120438]) and
nDCG@100 from 0.279253 to 0.365595 (difference 0.086342, CI
[0.078673, 0.094077]) against a frozen comparator. After confirmation, applying the winner unchanged to the full benchmark
permits a diagnosis of candidate exposure and ordering headroom. These results support interpreting retrieval performance
at the retriever–representation configuration level while keeping
development transfer, complete-system confirmation, and postconfirmatory diagnosis as distinct evidence levels.
Index Terms—patent retrieval, cross-domain retrieval, document representation, dense retrieval, information retrieval

I. I NTRODUCTION
A patent retriever never sees a patent. It sees the representation we choose to give it. When benchmark comparisons hold
the indexed representation fixed, lexical and dense retrievers
appear to receive the same evidence. This convention is useful,
but it embeds an assumption: that a fixed representation is a
model-independent control rather than part of the evaluated
system. Does fixing the representation actually make the
comparison model-independent?
Patent families make this tension concrete. The same invention may be exposed through a title and abstract, full claims,
a structured independent claim, overlapping passages, or separate section-specific views. Prior patent-retrieval studies have
already established that document granularity and text fields
affect effectiveness, particularly across technological domains
[1], [2]. The stronger question is whether a representationsource ordering observed with one retriever remains stable
when another retriever consumes the same representations.
We study this question by freezing five heterogeneous
retrievers and varying deterministic representations without
fine-tuning model weights. We first measure their response
to a common representation surface and to per-system representation search. We then transfer representations among

three dense retrievers and ask: best representation—for whom?
The nominal source ranking changes across consumers, while
the small within-target ranges and strong target-system pattern
show that retriever identity remains central to absolute quality.
The resulting claim is about the portability of representation
choices, not a large representation effect.
The evidence is organized in three stages that answer
different questions. Development analysis characterizes representation response and cross-retriever portability. A onetime selection stage and protected 872-query evaluation then
test a complete configuration at the system level. Only after
that system is fixed do we use its full-benchmark Top-200
pool for post-confirmatory diagnosis. Figure 1 maps this
evidence sequence and its development boundary. The primary
empirical contribution is controlled multi-retriever evidence on
representation response and transfer, with protected selection
and held-out confirmation providing system-level validation.
After the winner is fixed, candidate exposure and ordering
headroom are characterized in an additional, scope-limited
diagnostic follow-up.
II. R ELATED W ORK
Patent retrieval benchmarks increasingly expose the effects
of domain and document construction. DAPFAM provides
family-level in-domain and out-of-domain evaluation and systematically compares lexical and dense retrieval, document and
passage units, aggregation, and fusion [1]. PatenTEB broadens
patent embedding evaluation across retrieval and other patentlanguage tasks, while a recent 22-model benchmark examines
multiple patent text-section views and reports persistent out-ofdomain degradation [2], [3]. This work establishes that model
family and document view both affect patent retrieval. We
study whether a representation selected around one frozen retriever remains portable to another consumer under a protocol
that separates development, selection, and confirmation.
Document-representation optimization is a closely related
line of work. AutoIndex searches executable programs that
slice, enrich, normalize, and reorganize documents around a
fixed primary BM25 backend [4]. Our study uses deterministic
representation search as a controlled evaluation surface, then
changes the consumer: representations selected around one
retriever are transferred to other frozen retrievers in a complete
matrix. The methodological contribution lies in this portability
test and its separation from later system-level confirmation,
rather than in a new representation-search algorithm. The
protected stage evaluates the selected complete configuration

Multi-stage retrieval systems distinguish candidate generation from downstream scoring: an efficient first stage supplies
a bounded candidate set, after which more expensive models
extract features or rerank those candidates [5], [6]. We use this
established distinction after confirmation, measuring candidate
exposure and computing a perfect-ordering bound within the
fixed Top-200 pool. The analysis separates evidence absent
from the pool from evidence that remains available for improved ordering.

IV. C ROSS -R ETRIEVER R EPRESENTATION T RANSFER
If representation were neutral preprocessing, would frozen
retrievers respond similarly when the representation changes?
The common screen evaluates five systems under five shared
constructions, for 25 logical development cells. PatEmbed,
Arctic Embed, and Qwen3 advance to transfer analysis; BM25
and BGE-M3 remain diagnostic. The screen measures a predefined development surface on one benchmark and is not
intended as a universal model leaderboard.
Per-system search considers 52 prespecified configurations.
Forty-four are evaluated, while eight conditional reserves
remain dormant because their activation predicates are not
triggered; all executed runs complete successfully. The responses are mixed. Arctic shows a strict improvement under
its decision rule. PatEmbed produces a unique within-search
winner that is numerically tied to its incumbent under the
frozen comparison rule. Qwen3 yields no strict improvement,
while BM25 and BGE-M3 end in diagnostic ties without a
per-system winner. The common screen and per-system search
use different decision rules, so their displayed values have no
causal before/after interpretation. Table II reports these distinct
development summaries and their frozen interpretations.

The mixed responses leave open the possibility of a generally useful representation. A representation discovered around
one system might still transfer reliably to the others. We test
this possibility with a complete 3 × 3 matrix: each representation selected from PatEmbed, Arctic Embed, or Qwen3 is
consumed by each of the three target retrievers. Every cell
covers all 250 development queries.
Figure 2 shows that the nominally highest-scoring representation source changes with the target retriever. Recall@100 is
highest when the Qwen3-derived representation is consumed
by PatEmbed (0.419274), off the PatEmbed diagonal. The
Arctic-derived representation is nominally highest for Arctic
(0.341341), whereas the PatEmbed-derived representation is
nominally highest for Qwen3 (0.362570). The nDCG@100
ordering is also non-universal: its highest cell is the Arcticderived representation with PatEmbed (0.352416). No source
therefore retains the top rank across all consuming retrievers.
Target identity remains strongly associated with quality:
all three representations score near 0.419 Recall@100 with
PatEmbed, versus roughly 0.338–0.341 with Arctic and 0.359–
0.363 with Qwen3. The matrix therefore shows limited portability of representation rankings while preserving a strong
target-retriever pattern in absolute performance.
The within-target Recall@100 ranges are narrow: 0.000838
for PatEmbed, 0.003911 for Arctic, and 0.003073 for Qwen3.
Representation choice therefore changes the nominal ordering
at the configuration level without producing a large withintarget effect. In a secondary composition control, two-system
fusion (0.418715) slightly exceeds the strongest single system
(0.418436), whereas three-system fusion falls to 0.415084;
adding retrievers does not improve quality monotonically.
As descriptive development evidence, the transfer analysis addresses the portability of representation choices across
frozen retrievers. We next move to a separate system-level

question: whether a configuration selected under the frozen
development protocol retains its advantage on held-out data.
V. P ROTECTED C ONFIRMATION AND F ULL -S CALE
D IAGNOSIS
A. Protected Held-Out Confirmation
Four frozen profiles enter the one-time Selection-125 evaluation, and all produce complete rankings. Cross-domain
selection statistics use the 90 queries with positive crossdomain relations. The preregistered rule identifies the research
configuration, while the static comparator is the predefined
comparison system. Once Selection-125 closes, these two
complete systems and their model, representation, prompt,
program, and runtime bindings are fixed before Final-872
access.
Both Final-872 systems process all 872 queries without
failure, and deterministic replay is verified. Figure 3 reports
absolute results, paired differences, and query-level Recall
outcomes.
For Recall@100, the selected configuration scores 0.442476
versus 0.331097, a paired difference of 0.111379 with 95%
CI [0.102294, 0.120438]. It wins on 619 queries, ties on
158, and loses on 95. nDCG@100 increases from 0.279253
to 0.365595, a difference of 0.086342 with CI [0.078673,
0.094077]. nDCG@10 increases descriptively from 0.233666
to 0.297459 (difference 0.063794); the frozen evidence provides no canonical confidence interval for this endpoint.
Development and selection are closed before these outcomes
are observed. The selected complete configuration retains
its advantage across Recall@100 and nDCG@100, with a
favorable Recall outcome on 619 of 872 queries. This held-out
paired comparison provides the paper’s strongest confirmatory
evidence and supports the frozen system as a whole. Its design
does not separate the contribution of representation from the
other fixed components.

B. Full-Benchmark Candidate Exposure
Final-872 establishes that the selected complete configuration retains its advantage on held-out data. With that comparison settled, we keep the winner fixed and ask what constrains
the cross-domain performance that remains. The unchanged
system is applied to the complete benchmark—45,336 target
families, 1,247 query families, and Top-200 retrieval for every
query—to create a stable diagnostic pool. The run materializes 188,944 chunks and 249,400 ranked family rows with
100% coverage, zero failures, and no duplicate-family errors.
The strict cross-domain population differs from Final-872, so
these values are reported only as a separate full-benchmark
characterization.
This fixed pool permits a bounded diagnostic question:
How much of the remaining error can ordering within the
existing candidates possibly recover? Under the strict crossdomain definition used for this post-confirmatory analysis,
905 judged queries contain 5,193 relevant-family incidences.
Observed macro Recall@100 is 0.188450, while Recall@200
is 0.260167.
Figure 4 separates relevant-family incidence exposure from
query-averaged recall. Of 5,193 strict cross-domain relevant
incidences, 796 occur by rank 100, 332 first appear at ranks
101–200, and 4,065 do not occur in the Top-200 pool. The
query view is consistent with this limited exposure: 67 of 905
queries have all relevant families exposed by rank 100, 297
have partial exposure by rank 100, 86 first expose a relevant
family at ranks 101–200, and 455 expose none by rank 200.
Perfectly ordering the existing pool would place every available relevant family as early as possible, raising macro Recall@100 from 0.188450 to 0.260167. The resulting 0.071717
headroom quantifies the opportunity for improved ordering
inside this pool. The larger incidence count outside Top-200
identifies a separate exposure constraint for the confirmed
system, beyond the reach of a downstream ranker restricted
to these candidates.

VI. D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSION
The study supports a configuration-level unit of interpretation. A retrieval model scores the evidence made available by
document construction, section and view selection, segmentation, and family-level aggregation. These choices should be
reported with precision comparable to the model checkpoint
and retrieval depth. Retriever identity remains essential to the
system; representation specifies the evidence on which that
retriever acts.
The transfer matrix makes this conclusion more precise. The
nominal source ranking changes across consuming retrievers,
while the small within-target ranges and separated target score
bands show that retriever identity remains strongly associated
with absolute performance. This combination is informative:
the representation ranking varies across consuming retrievers
even when local numerical separation is modest. The result
strengthens a configuration-level interpretation without assigning a large effect to representation alone.
The protected evaluation shifts the level of inference from
transfer behavior to the complete retrieval system. Final872 supports the selected frozen configuration under heldout evaluation, with development and selection already closed.
Keeping this distinction explicit allows the development matrix
to inform interpretation while the paired held-out comparison
carries the confirmatory claim.
Confirmation also provides a stable system to interrogate. The fixed-pool diagnosis separates candidate exposure
from ordering among retrieved candidates. Reranking experiments can evaluate gains within an unchanged pool, whereas
candidate-expansion experiments can test whether first-stage
retrieval supplies additional relevant families. Treating these
questions separately will clarify which intervention changes
exposure and which changes ordering; the present analysis
does not predict their relative effectiveness.
